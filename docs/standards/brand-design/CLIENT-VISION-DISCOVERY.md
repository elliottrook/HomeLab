# Client vision discovery and interpretation

Use for a new client/identity, broad redesign or changed ethos. For a precise edit, reuse the approved brief and ask only what changed or is missing. The aim is to help a client express meaning, desired impressions and taste without requiring design vocabulary. Preferences may develop during exploration; this is not mind-reading or psychological diagnosis.

## Run the opening

Confirm name, business activity and main customers briefly; perform essential risk/rights checks. Say: “You don't need to know anything about design. I'll ask about your business and a few things you respond to, then show you some possibilities. There are no right answers.”

Ask one question at a time. Start with stories and impressions (1–5); select later prompts where useful rather than administering all ten. Capture answers in the engagement's `DISCOVERY_BRIEF.md`, instantiated from [the project template](../../templates/brand-design/DISCOVERY_BRIEF.template.md) or the installed skill's bundled fallback. Skip already answered questions. Do not mistake this for an audience study.

1. **Desired reputation:** “Imagine a customer recommending you to a friend. How would you love them to finish: ‘Go to [brand] because…’?” Follow with an example of what would give a customer reason to say that. Record this as an aspiration, not an actual testimonial.
2. **Story:** “Tell me about a job or moment that made you think, ‘That's what we're about.’ What happened, and what made it matter?” For a new business, ask about an experience they want it to embody.
3. **First impression:** “Someone sees your van, sign or homepage before meeting you. What do you hope they think you're like? What would you hate them to assume?” Use the relevant surface, not a forced industry example.
4. **Priorities:** “Give me a few words—up to ten—that you'd like people to use about the business. Which three matter most? Which words would be completely wrong?” Ask what each priority means to them.
5. **Boundaries:** “Finish: ‘We're ___, but not ___.’ Which side would it be easy for a designer to take too far?” Let them supply the terms before offering examples.
6. **Associations:** “Show me something you like the look or feel of. It needn't be a logo: a place, object, package, album cover, vehicle or clothing is fine. What draws you to it? What wouldn't you borrow?” Record transferable qualities, not permission to copy.
7. **Taste versus fit:** “Show me something that looks good to you but would be wrong for this company. What makes it wrong?”
8. **Optional person metaphor:** “If the business were a person meeting a new customer, how would that meeting go? How would they speak, behave and make the customer feel?” Skip if unnatural; discuss an actual meeting instead.
9. **Visual elicitation:** “Which of these pictures feels closest to the business you want people to encounter? What part feels right? What part feels wrong?” Use a small, varied set of non-logo references (spaces, materials, lettering, colours); allow none, combinations and new references. Ask for explanation before presenting your interpretation. Identify references and selected details precisely.
10. **Direction check, after showing a possibility:** “If we make it look this way, what might people misunderstand about you? What would you keep, change or add?”

Useful follow-ups: “What about that?” “Can you give me an example?” “What does that word mean to you?” “Is that something you personally enjoy, or how you want the business to come across—or both?” “Is that true today, something you want to grow into, or a mixture?” Unpack generic terms such as professional, trustworthy and quality through examples; do not silently substitute your own definition.

### Optional word cards

Ask for unprompted words first. If stuck, offer a mixed bank and blanks; sort into feels like us / not us / unsure, then choose three priorities and three impressions to avoid. Example bank: approachable, reserved, bold, understated, playful, serious, traditional, modern, polished, rugged, precise, expressive, calm, energetic, familiar, unusual, exclusive, everyday, intimidating, bland, fussy, cold, showy, careless. It is not a validated personality instrument. Do not offer only flattering words, force binary choices or assume words mean the same thing to everyone.

## Interpret without inventing

1. **Capture evidence:** Keep exact client words with source/date; distinguish quoted answers from paraphrases. Do not manufacture anecdotes, audience reactions, biographies or missing answers. Record unanswered items as unknown.
2. **Separate dimensions:** Tag current identity versus aspiration, personal taste versus desired customer impression, and constraints versus preferences. Preserve mixtures and tensions; don't flatten them into one adjective.
3. **Find priorities:** Identify the client's top qualities, unwanted impressions, distinctive examples and repeated themes. Weight explicit ranking and explained examples above isolated ambiguous adjectives. Ask about contradictions rather than silently resolving them.
4. **Form tentative hypotheses:** For each proposed meaning, record supporting evidence, alternative interpretations and an open question where needed. No fixed dictionary such as trustworthy = blue, playful = pink, welding = sparks. Do not apply another brand's palette or ethos by default.
5. **Translate into visual possibilities:** Link the interpretation to possible typography, geometry, colour, composition, imagery, spacing or motion. Offer materially different plausible routes and state what each tests and risks. These are designer deductions, not client instructions or proven audience effects.
6. **Read back:** “I'm hearing X, Y and Z, but not A or B. One possibility is ___. Another is ___. What have I misunderstood?” Record corrections and confirmation source. Brief confirmation is not approval of artwork; artwork approval remains version/hash-specific.
7. **Use visual feedback early:** Show modest reference comparisons or sketches once there is enough information; don't wait for every question. Keep content/context comparable. Explain which decisions remain open. Ask what fits, what does not and what causes that reaction. Formal concept count and master approval follow the existing workflow.
8. **Converge and resume:** Update the brief with confirmed decisions and rejected directions/reasons. Preserve approved elements and change only the agreed scope. Record unresolved issues and next question in `PROJECT_STATE.md`. Promote only confirmed durable decisions into `BRAND-BRIEF.md`; retain tentative evidence in working records.

Owner approval establishes intent/preference, not what customers actually perceive. If audience reception needs verification, propose a separate appropriately scoped audience test; do not present an agent's judgement as empirical evidence.

## Later practical intake

After the emotional opening and synthesis, ask only practical questions needed for the task:

- Exact legal/display name, capitalization, initials, tagline and literal copy; decision-maker and approval process.
- Main audiences, services, positioning and relevant competitors; whether owner aspirations fit actual customers.
- Deliverables and placements: logo variants, website scope, social, print, watermark, signage, favicon/icon sizes and export formats.
- Existing masters, elements to preserve, exclusions, brand relationships and approved asset-store paths.
- Accessibility, legibility, production/renderer constraints, typography and licensing, cultural/personal cues, consent/provenance and privacy.
- Website needs when applicable: visitor tasks, content/photos and stories, navigation, calls to action, responsive behaviour, accessibility and integrations. Route unrelated site implementation to the appropriate workflow; this skill does not expand authorization.
- Budget/timing if relevant; release destination, publication/deployment approval and maintenance ownership.

Keep urgent rights/privacy/authorization constraints early. Do not make a missing nonessential future deliverable prevent an exploratory local draft. All existing promotion/publication gates remain intact.

## Example: hypothetical Smith Welding

Evidence: “People bring us awkward repairs”; “we explain what can and can't be fixed”; “proper workmanship, not a big faceless contractor.” Likes an old workshop sign's straightforward lettering but dislikes distressing.

Tentative interpretation: capable, candid, locally approachable; clarity without corporate distance. Confirm it. Possible route A: a sturdy readable wordmark with a restrained custom joining detail. Route B: a clean workshop-style badge without artificial ageing. Ask what feels right/wrong and why. These answers do not establish a colour or require sparks. This is a fictional teaching example, not a real client record.

## Rationale and method limits

These prompts are an adapted discovery procedure, not a scientifically validated questionnaire. Stories, explained visual selections and ranked words support discussion; neutral wording reduces interviewer suggestion.

- [IDEO.org Card Sort](https://www.designkit.org/methods/card-sort.html): word/image sorting and discussion of priorities and values.
- [IDEO.org Collage](https://www.designkit.org/methods/collage.html): explained visual selections can surface themes; the brief picture prompt here is an adaptation, not the full method.
- [NN/G Desirability Toolkit](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/microsoft-desirability-toolkit/): mixed positive/negative/neutral reaction vocabulary for existing designs. Applying it to owner aspirations is an adaptation, not audience measurement.
- [NN/G Leading Questions](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/leading-questions/): avoid wording that supplies the desired answer.

The owner's reviewed brand chats informed the proposed meaning → visual reaction → controlled refinement sequence. That deduction is case-informed, not proof of universal effectiveness. Private transcripts and identifiers are intentionally not bundled in this reusable skill.
